# Now

The Cabinet of Clocks has fourteen drawers, each a live clock with a sourced page. Seven cut up the day by the clock, the eighth (the age of the Moon) counts the month, and the ninth to eleventh (unequal hours; Italian and Babylonian hours; Japanese temporal hours) cut the day by the Sun at a place, Rome (Tokyo for the Japanese clock) unless the reader enters their own. The twelfth, the Maya Long Count, counts days since 3114 BC, with the tzolk'in and haab' and the count drawn in bar-and-dot numerals. The thirteenth, the Roman watches, cuts the night from sunset to sunrise into four watches (at Rome unless the reader enters a place). The fourteenth, the French Republican calendar, gives the day, décade day, rural day name and year of the Republic, with the year starting on the day of the September equinox at Paris. A "one moment" page (`/moment/`) stops every drawer at a date and time the reader picks, and a "one day" page (`/day/`) lays the four Sun clocks out as bands across one local day, for any date and place. The cabinet is working toward a collection broad enough that the front page reads as a survey of how people have cut up time.

Next steps, roughly in order:

- The Roman day was also named in parts (mane, meridies, vespera); a drawer or note for the named parts of the civil day could come later.

Unfinished or worth checking:

- The Republican calendar follows the equinox rule; an option for Delambre's fixed rule (which Wikipedia's own date box uses, and which differs by a day in 2026) could come later. Its equinox is good to about 2 minutes; Year 301 (2092) falls within seconds of midnight. Its sub-line is cut off in the front-page drawer, as other long sub-lines are.

- The Maya clock uses the GMT correlation (584,283) and turns the day at local midnight; an option for Thompson's 584,285, or the nine Lords of the Night, could come later. Its numerals draw the b'ak'tun place modulo 20, which only matters after the year 4772.

- The one-moment page's Sun and Moon readings use present-era formulas; far from now they drift, as the page says. More suggested moments could be added (each needs a sourced UTC time), and any new drawer appears there automatically.

- The ship's bells clock uses the plain count through the dog watches; navies differ, and a note or option for the other pattern could come later.
- The sidereal clock is mean, not apparent, sidereal time; fine for looking up, not for instruments, as the about page says.
- The moon clock's phase names call a principal phase within 6° of elongation (about half a day either side); the lit fraction uses (1 − cos E)/2 with the elongation, a close approximation of the phase angle.
- The unequal-hours clock counts the night in twelve hours too; the night watches are now drawer No. 13.
- The watches clock takes each watch as exactly a quarter of the night; the Hebrew three-watch night and the Jewish fixed-time version (sunset to 10 pm, and so on) are described, not shown. The sunrise/sunset line now rounds to the minute (session 6).
- The Italian-hours clock counts from sunset, not from the end of dusk half an hour later, which some places used. Wikipedia's Lugano example (December sunrise in the 14th hour) does not match the clock and was left out; worth finding out why.
- The Japanese clock starts each hour at sunrise, noon or sunset, as the English source says; some accounts centre the hours on those moments instead, and some started the day at dawn rather than sunrise. A note or option could come later.
- On the large dial, "Night hour VIII" on the unequal-hours clock is probably too wide and gets cut off; worth checking.
- The one-day page scrolls sideways on phones and its row labels scroll away with it; sticky labels (an HTML column beside the SVG) would help. Its "night about" length is 24 hours minus the daylight, so it is slightly off on clock-change days and in the polar regions' last days of rising and setting. Very narrow hours (a summer night's unequal hour near the polar circles) lose their labels.
- The front-page drawer for Italian hours wraps its sub-line to two lines on narrow drawers.
